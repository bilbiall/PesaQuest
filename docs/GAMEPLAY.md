## 1. Executive Summary

PesaQuest is a **virtual financial life simulator**. Instead of reading about budgeting,
a learner *lives* a compressed financial life inside a fictional Kenyan city — **Pesa
City** — using pretend money (virtual Kenyan Shillings). They study, get hired, collect
salaries, pay rent, save at a bank that pays real compound interest, buy income-earning
assets, borrow and repay loans, survive economic shocks, and watch a **credit score** and
**net worth** respond honestly to every decision.

The core theory of change: **financial behaviour is learned by consequence, not by
lecture.** In PesaQuest every mistake is survivable and every consequence is explained —
a missed bill lowers the learner's credit score *and* tells them why; a lottery-style
shortcut does not exist; wealth only comes from the same behaviours that build wealth in
real life: earning, budgeting, saving, investing and patience.

Key facts for reviewers:

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Who is it for? | Ages 8–12, 13–17, 18–25, 26+ (content adapts per band) |
| What money is used? | 100% virtual shillings. No real money enters or leaves gameplay |
| Is there gambling? | No. No mechanic converts chance into gameplay currency advantage |
| Is it Kenyan? | Entirely — M-Pesa, chamas, boda gigs, NSE, PAYE/NHIF/NSSF, matatus |
| Can teachers use it? | Yes — school accounts let a teacher manage a class (see §16) |
| Cost | Free to start with a full trial; optional subscription affects pace, never access to learning |

## 2. What PesaQuest Is (and Is Not)

**It is a simulation, not a quiz.** There are no multiple-choice tests standing between a
learner and progress. Knowledge is exercised by *doing*: to learn about interest a player
opens a savings account and watches interest arrive month after month; to learn about
default they experience what a missed loan payment does to their borrowing power.

**It is systemic, not scripted.** The world runs on interacting systems (time, income,
expenses, markets, random events). Two learners will have different financial lives
depending on their choices — exactly the property that makes reflection and classroom
discussion productive ("Why is your credit score 710 and mine 480?").

**It is safe.** The worst possible outcome in PesaQuest is a virtual bad month. Learners
can fail, reflect and rebuild — a rehearsal space for the highest-stakes life skill there
is.

**It is not:** a gambling product, a trading app, a real-money platform, an advertising
vehicle, or a chat platform with strangers (see §21).

## 3. Getting Started

1. **Registration** requires a name, email, password and date of birth.
2. From the date of birth the game assigns one of four **age groups** — 8–12, 13–17,
   18–25, 26+ — which tunes content difficulty, the bills a player receives, the life
   events that can occur, and the tone of the writing.
3. Every new account starts as **"The Student"** life chapter with a small virtual
   balance, a clean credit score of 500 (on a 300–850 scale), and a short onboarding that
   introduces the dashboard and the city.
4. A **career quiz** (interests-based, ~5 questions) suggests one of ten career paths —
   technology, healthcare, finance, creative, education, agriculture, media, engineering,
   law, business. The path guides which courses and jobs are *recommended*; it never
   restricts choice. Notably, choosing a career gives **no money** — a deliberate lesson
   that ambition without skills pays nothing.

**Safeguarding at signup:** no phone number is required to play, no identity documents,
no payment information. For under-13 learners the recommended route is a school-managed
account created by a teacher (see §16).

## 4. A Guided Tour: A Learner's First Session

The fastest way to understand PesaQuest is to walk through a typical first hour.

1. **The Dashboard** greets the player with their character panel: balance, level,
   credit score, mood, and the door into **Pesa City**.
2. **Entering Pesa City** shows a hand-illustrated city map with districts to explore.
   A guided first mission points them to the **Opportunity Hub**.
3. **At the Opportunity Hub** they browse starter courses — e.g. *"Money Basics: Needs,
   Wants & the 50/30/20 Rule"*. Enrolling opens a **course reader** with actual teaching
   content written for their age band. Completing it awards experience points (XP) and —
   crucially — **unlocks jobs** that required it.
4. **Getting hired.** With a certificate earned, they apply for their first job — say
   *Posho Mill Attendant at Wamalwa Millers, KES 9,000/month, part-time*. A welcome
   notification explains the salary and the rule that will shape their play: **pay must
   be collected by reporting to work.**
5. **Time passes.** As real time passes, game days tick by (see §6). Bills appear on
   their Life HQ board with due dates. A payslip becomes ready at work.
6. **The loop locks in.** The player returns, sees a *"While You Were Away"* summary of
   what happened in their absence, reports to work to collect pay, pays their bills
   before the due dates, moves spare cash into savings, and checks the quest board for
   their next goal.

That loop — **earn → obligate → save → invest → progress** — is the whole curriculum,
practised over and over with rising stakes as the player's virtual life grows.

## 5. The Game World: Pesa City

Pesa City is a full-screen illustrated city map. Each district is a financial concept a
learner can walk into:

| District | Gameplay | What it teaches |
|---|---|---|
| 🎓 **Opportunity Hub** | Enroll in courses (some free, some paid); apply for jobs and gigs | Education is an investment; qualifications unlock income |
| 🏢 **Workplace District** | View active jobs; **Report to Work** to collect pending pay; resign | Reliability; income follows showing up |
| 🏦 **The Bank** | Open savings goals; deposit/withdraw; take loans; view interest earned | Compound interest; the discipline of separating savings from spending money |
| 📈 **Equity Square** | Time-bound investment deals with stated risk levels and maturity dates | Risk vs return; illiquidity; diversification |
| 🛒 **Marketplace** | Buy income-generating assets (from a thermos flask business to apartments) | Assets vs liabilities; payback period; running costs |
| 🚗 **Jua Kali Car Yard** / 🏘️ **Kiambu Estates** | Big-ticket assets purchasable with a deposit + financing | Deposits, loan interest, total cost of credit |
| 🎡 **Fun World** | Leisure activities (cinema, nyama choma, Karura walk) that cost money and restore mood | Recreation is a budget line, not a moral failure |
| 📜 **Quest Board** | Accept structured goals with XP/cash rewards | Goal-setting with deadlines |

The map also surfaces **world events** — contextual pop-up situations tied to the
player's career and belongings — plus rotating financial tips ("hustle tips") written by
the content team.

## 6. How Game Time Works

PesaQuest runs on an accelerated clock so that long-horizon lessons (compound interest,
loan terms, asset appreciation) fit inside a school term.

- **One "game day" is the basic unit** (internally called a *tick*).
- Administrators set the pace — e.g., *one real day = one game week*. At that setting a
  game month passes in roughly four real days.
- **Time only "settles" when the player returns.** Nothing punishes a learner in real
  time while they're at school; instead, on their next visit the game replays the days
  they missed: bills fell due, salary became payable, savings earned interest, assets
  appreciated, and perhaps a random life event occurred.
- The **"While You Were Away"** summary presents those days as a short story with
  colour-coded items — money earned in green, obligations and losses in red, milestones
  highlighted — each with an expandable financial lesson.
- **Catch-up is capped** (maximum two game months per return) so a learner who was away
  for holidays returns to a digestible situation, not a ruined life.
- Deliberately, absence has a cost (see §8): the game does **not** reward inactivity
  with accumulated riches. This design choice was made specifically so the simulation
  cannot be "won" by leaving it alone.

*For reviewers: the pace is a server-side setting, so a school pilot can run a slower or
faster economy without any change to the app.*

## 7. The Player's Character

Six meters make the learner's financial state visible at all times. Each one is a
teaching instrument:

| Meter | Range / form | What moves it | The lesson embedded |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wallet (Ksh)** | virtual shillings | income in, spending out | Cash is what you can spend *today* |
| **Net Worth** | cash + assets + savings − debts | everything | Wealth ≠ cash; you can be cash-poor and wealth-rich |
| **Credit Score** | 300–850, starts 500 | bill & loan behaviour, saving habits (see §12) | Reputation is built slowly and lost quickly |
| **XP & Level** | points → levels with names ("Novice" → "PesaQuest Legend") | courses, quests, jobs, daily play | Effort compounds too |
| **Mood** | 0–100 | decays daily; restored by leisure | Burnout is expensive: low mood cuts work income 10% |
| **Life Chapter** | Student → Graduate → Hustler → Settler → Builder → Elder | net-worth milestones | A visible long-term arc: wealth stages of a Kenyan life |

Everything the player does eventually shows up in one of these six numbers — which is
what makes progress discussable, comparable and assessable (§22).

## 8. Earning

### Courses → Jobs (skills before salary)
Jobs are gated behind **courses**. Each course has genuine written teaching content
(read inside the game, at the learner's pace) and a completion step. Some advanced jobs
require *two* certificates. This hard link — study first, earn after — is the game's most
repeated lesson.

### Three kinds of work
Every job is classified, and the classification carries rules the player feels:

| Type | Rule | The lesson |
|---|---|---|
| 🏢 **Full-time** | The player's *only* job. Best salaries. Must resign to change | Commitment trades flexibility for stability |
| ⏰ **Part-time** | Up to **two** at once | Juggling incomes is possible but each pays less |
| ⚡ **Freelance gig** | One-off: work ~7 game days, get paid **once** on delivery; the same gig only reopens after 4 game weeks | Gig income is real but lumpy and cannot be farmed — plan around it |

### Payday: Report to Work
Salaries are **never deposited automatically**. Each game month the player's pay becomes
a *pending payslip*; the player must actively **Report to Work** (one tap at the
Workplace, Career page or Life HQ) to bank it. Uncollected paychecks **stack safely** —
no money is ever lost. The pressure is disciplinary instead: every payday that lands on
top of an uncollected one counts as a **missed collection (a "strike")**.

- **3 strikes in a row** → the employer issues a **final notice** (bell + push + the
  While-You-Were-Away screen, and a red banner on the Career page).
- **One more game month of silence** after the notice → **dismissal**: the job is gone,
  mood −15, credit −15, and the player must re-apply through the Opportunity Hub. The
  earned wages remain collectible as a final settlement — the lesson targets showing up,
  never confiscation.
- Reporting to work at any point wipes the strike count clean.

The next payday is always visible: a **💰 Payday in Xd** chip sits on the calendar HUD,
payday markers appear on the game calendar, and the dashboard's **This Week's Money**
strip forecasts the week's cash in vs out.

Two modifiers apply at collection: low mood (below 40) docks 10%, and an active economic
crisis with a salary cut docks more (§14).

*Worked example a reviewer can trace:* Amina takes the free *Money Basics* course →
qualifies for Posho Mill Attendant (part-time, KES 9,000/mo) → 30 game days later her
payslip is ready → she is away for three game months → on return she finds KES 27,000
stacked and waiting, but also a **final notice**: "You've skipped 3 paydays in a row —
report to work within the next game month or you will be dismissed." She reports to
work, banks all KES 27,000, and her attendance slate resets.

## 9. Obligations: Bills & Budgeting

- Bills are assigned automatically as the virtual life grows: age-appropriate basics
  first (e.g. airtime, transport), then consequences of ownership — buy a motorbike and
  insurance + fuel bills follow; buy property and a service charge follows.
- Each bill repeats on a fixed cycle (e.g. every 30 game days) and appears on the **Life
  HQ Bills Board** with a live countdown.
- **All bills are paid manually.** There is no autopay. Paying on time earns a small
  credit-score reward. Missing the due date flips the bill to **overdue**, damages the
  credit score, and keeps damaging it each full cycle it stays unpaid (with a cap per
  visit so absence is never ruinous).
- Paying an overdue bill repairs only **half** the normal credit reward — being late is
  survivable but never free.
- **Billing cycles stay anchored to their calendar**: pay 5 days early and the next bill
  is due in 35 days; pay 5 days late and the next is due in 25. A learner cannot stretch
  a month by paying late — exactly like a real landlord or utility.

The bills board also explains each bill ("Why this bill?"), its consequence if missed,
and the repeat cycle — budgeting taught as a living list rather than a worksheet.

## 10. Saving & Compound Interest

The bank offers **savings goals** (e.g. "Emergency fund", "New phone"). Money moved into
savings leaves the spending wallet — the classic "pay yourself first" separation.

- Savings earn **compound interest, credited monthly**, at an annual rate set by
  administrators (default 8%, in the range of real Kenyan bank/SACCO rates).
- The account statement separates **deposits** from **interest earned**, so a learner can
  literally watch money their money made: *"Deposited 5,000 · Interest earned 66."*
- Withdrawals are allowed any time (with a gentle educational warning) — teaching that
  liquidity is a feature of savings, unlike the locked investment deals in §11.
- Consistent saving behaviour is one of the credit score's positive signals (§12).

## 11. Assets & Investing

The Marketplace sells **productive assets** with honest economics:

- Every asset shows **price, monthly income, monthly running cost** — and the game
  computes the **payback period** ("pays itself off in ~7 game months") so learners
  compare like investors, not shoppers.
- Assets **decay** (condition drops monthly) and need paid maintenance; neglect reduces
  their output. Ownership has running responsibilities.
- Asset values drift over time — most appreciate slowly; a market crisis can knock them
  down (§14). Net worth breathes.
- **Luxury items exist deliberately** — flashy purchases with no income. Nothing stops a
  learner buying one; their net worth simply tells the truth afterwards.
- **Investment deals** at Equity Square lock money until a maturity date with a stated
  risk level — return is not guaranteed. **Financing** exists for vehicles/property:
  a deposit plus a monthly loan, with the interest cost displayed before signing.

## 12. Borrowing & the Credit Score

Loans are presented as a tool, not a trap or a taboo. Installments auto-deduct monthly
(like a real bank standing order), interest compounds, early repayment saves money — and
everything feeds the credit score:

| Behaviour | Credit effect |
|---|---|
| Bill paid on time | small + |
| Bill overdue (per cycle) | − |
| Clearing an overdue bill | + (half) |
| Loan installment paid / missed | +5 / −20 |
| Loan fully repaid / defaulted | +30 / −100 |
| Consistent savings habit | +10 |
| Holding assets responsibly | +5 |
| Job loyalty | +10 |
| Wallet hitting zero | −10 |
| Reaching a net-worth chapter | +15 |

Every change is logged with its reason in a **credit history** panel the learner can
read — the score is never mysterious. The scale (300–850) matches real credit reference
bureaus so the mental model transfers directly to adult life.

## 13. Quests: Structured Learning Goals

Quests are the game's lesson plans: concrete goals with XP (and sometimes small cash)
rewards, gated by level so difficulty rises with competence.

- **Auto-completing:** the game detects real achievements — "Save Ksh 3,000",
  "Complete Boda Road Safety AND get hired as a courier", "Buy your first asset" — and
  celebrates the moment they happen. No self-reporting, no honor system.
- **Multi-step quests** chain behaviours (course → job → savings threshold), teaching
  that financial outcomes are sequences, not single acts.
- Every quest carries a written **lesson** shown at completion.
- A configurable **daily quest cap** prevents binge-completing; learning is paced across
  days — deliberate spaced repetition.

### City Contracts — the self-generating quest layer
Alongside authored quests, every player receives **City Contracts**: timed, multi-part
missions issued by Pesa City's NPC cast — **Mama Pesa** 🧺 (bills & savings discipline),
**Bazu** ⚡ (hustle & side money), **Madam Rita** 📋 (careers & study), **Mzee Kobe** 🐢
(assets & patient wealth) and **Shiko** 📣 (friends & community). Contracts are generated
from the player's *own situation* — overdue bills draw a rescue mission from Mama Pesa,
uncollected pay draws a payday errand, low mood draws a Fun World nudge — with targets
sized as ratios of the player's own numbers, so difficulty self-tunes at every wealth
level. Each contract bundles 3–5 objectives (drawn from 15 archetypes) and completes
when **all** or **any N** are done, per admin-tunable rules by age group and level band
(GameSet → Quests → 🤖 Automation). Copy comes from per-age-band voice packs — Sheng
for teens, playful for 8–12, drier wit for adults — so contracts read like errands from
characters, never like generated tasks. Progress is measured automatically wherever the
player plays; finished contracts pay XP + cash, expired ones simply fade and new ones
arrive. The result: the game always has something personal to do, with **zero ongoing
content authoring**.

## 14. Life Events & Economic Crises

**Life events** are randomized, age/chapter-appropriate happenings that arrive as game
days pass — fuel price hikes, a chama dividend, a relative asking for help, an NSE rally.
Each shows a Kenyan-flavoured quote, its financial effect, and an educational note. They
teach the deepest budgeting truth: *the unexpected is normal — plan for it.*

**Financial crises** are scheduled, server-wide economic shocks (an NSE crash, drought
inflation, a recession with salary cuts) controlled by the education team:

1. Every player receives a **warning notification before the crisis hits** (typically
   48 hours) — a chance to prepare: top up savings, avoid new debt.
2. When it hits, the effect applies to everyone — cash drains, asset values fall, or
   salaries are cut for the duration.
3. The event lands permanently on each player's **Life Story timeline** with a lesson
   ("Economic crises are why emergency funds exist…").

Pedagogically this is a **whole-class shared experience**: everyone weathers the same
storm with different preparation — ideal material for a follow-up classroom discussion.

## 15. Mood & Fun World

Mood (0–100) decays a point per game day. Fun World activities — a cinema trip, nyama
choma with friends, a free-ish forest walk — cost money and restore mood. Below 40 mood,
work income drops 10%.

This closes a loop most financial education ignores: **rest and recreation are budget
lines**. The learner discovers that never spending on themselves eventually costs money
(the salary penalty), while overspending on fun starves their goals — the balance *is*
the lesson. Activities span price points (KES 200 street food crawl to KES 5,000 road
trip) so budgeting for joy is a real choice.

## 16. Social Systems

The social layer follows one safety policy throughout: **public and moderated, or
private and structured — never private and free-form.** Players never exchange free-text
messages one-to-one; every 1-to-1 interaction is a choice between game-defined options.

- **Friends** (`/friends`) — players connect by **@username**, exact name, or a short
  **friend code** (PQ-XXXXXX; school accounts also see one-tap classmate suggestions).
  Requests must be accepted. Friendship unlocks lending and chama invites. Every player
  has a unique @username (auto-derived at signup, editable in Profile); profile share
  links use it (`/players/amina_k`) and chama links use the chama's name
  (`/chama/nairobi-investors`) — numeric IDs never appear in anything shareable.
- **Friend loans (P2P)** — negotiation with buttons, not chat: the borrower picks a
  preset **amount** and **repayment term** (10/20/30 game days) → the lender sees the
  borrower's credit label and offers a preset **rate** (5–20%) → the borrower accepts,
  or **counters once** at a lower preset, or declines → the lender accepts or declines.
  Money moves only on agreement; the due date lands on the borrower's calendar. Repaying
  on time earns credit (+10); past due, the game **garnishes** what cash exists and any
  shortfall becomes a **default** (credit −30) — the lender genuinely loses money, which
  teaches counterparty risk. Anti-farming rules: 5% interest floor, lenders risk at most
  20% of their cash, max 3 open loans per side, both players level 3+.
- **Chama** — players join group savings (merry-go-round) structures, propose and vote
  on group decisions: cooperative finance the Kenyan way. Chamas are now **public**
  (listed in the directory; the creator may set entry requirements — min level, credit
  score or savings) or **private** (invisible; entry by friend invite or a 6-character
  **join code** a teacher can write on the board). A player may belong to at most 3
  chamas; contribution windows appear on the game calendar.
- **Forums** — an X-style feed: **Hot / New / Top** tabs, ▲/▼ voting on discussions and
  replies (one vote each, tap again to undo, no self-votes), author cards with level,
  recent badges and **✦ karma** (net votes received — also a badge trigger). Level-gated
  for new accounts to deter spam; admin-moderated; school boards stay school-private.
- **Leaderboard** — celebrates net worth and progress; rankings reset context by age
  group.
- **School mode** — a school purchases a seat package; a teacher account manages
  members, sees the roster, and every seat plays with full features. This is the
  recommended deployment for classrooms and the route by which under-13 learners play
  under adult supervision.

## 17. Progression

- **XP Levels** with aspirational names ("Saver", "Investor", "Wealth Builder") gate
  quests and content.
- **Life Chapters** (§7) tell the macro story — net-worth milestones from Student to
  Elder, each with its own icon, tagline and celebration.
- **Badges** mark firsts and streaks (first job, first asset, savings streaks…).
- **Journey Milestones** show the road ahead (checklist of life goals).
- The **Life Story timeline** (`/life/timeline`) is the learner's autobiography: every
  event, crisis and chapter in order, each with its lesson — effectively a portfolio of
  their financial journey that a teacher can review with them.

## 18. Free Access vs Premium — the Equity Position

Moski is a non-profit; the subscription funds the platform. The design rule reviewers
should scrutinise is this: **no learning content is behind the paywall.**

- Free accounts play the entire loop: courses, jobs, bills, savings, credit, quests,
  events, crises.
- Premium affects **scale and pace**: how many game days simulate per visit, how many
  assets/savings goals/deals can be held at once, unlimited AI-coach questions, unlimited
  Fun World.
- Every new account starts with a **full-featured free trial**, and schools' seats are
  fully featured.
- All limits are administrator-configurable and can be relaxed platform-wide (e.g. a
  "free-for-all" switch used during school events).

## 19. Educational Design Principles

1. **Consequence over content.** Facts are embedded in outcomes. The game rarely says
   "don't miss payments"; it lets a missed payment cost 20 credit points and then
   explains itself.
2. **Safe failure, real feeling.** Losses are virtual but the *emotions* (the red
   overdue badge, the forfeited payslip) are genuine — affect drives retention.
3. **Effort-reward honesty.** There is no mechanic where luck or inactivity outperforms
   work, study and patience. The economy's maths quietly enforces the values it teaches.
4. **Kenyan authenticity.** M-Pesa floats, chamas, boda gigs, NSE tickers, PAYE/NHIF/
   NSSF deductions on payslips, matatu fares — learners recognise their own economy, so
   transfer to real life is direct.
5. **Spaced practice.** The clock forces skills to be *repeated* across days and weeks
   (every payday, every bill cycle) rather than demonstrated once.
6. **Visible arithmetic.** Interest, payback periods, loan costs and tax deductions are
   always shown as numbers the learner can check — the game is a word-problem generator
   with stakes.
7. **Reflection artifacts.** The timeline, credit history and net-worth chart give
   learners (and teachers) concrete objects to reflect on.
8. **Progressive depth.** The same city serves an 8-year-old learning needs-vs-wants and
   a 24-year-old comparing loan interest to asset yield.

## 20. CBC Alignment

PesaQuest was designed with Kenya's Competency Based Curriculum in mind. Financial
literacy is a recognised **Pertinent and Contemporary Issue (PCI)** in the CBC, and the
game addresses it through the curriculum's core competencies:

| CBC Core Competency | Where the game exercises it |
|---|---|
| **Critical thinking & problem solving** | Budget trade-offs; payback-period comparisons; crisis preparation; loan vs save decisions |
| **Self-efficacy** | Visible growth of credit score/net worth from the learner's own actions; recovery after setbacks |
| **Learning to learn** | In-game courses with completion steps; the course→job→income chain making the value of study concrete |
| **Digital literacy** | Safe navigation of a digital financial environment mirroring M-Pesa-era tools |
| **Citizenship** | Taxes on payslips (PAYE, NHIF, NSSF) presented as normal parts of working life; community chama finance |
| **Communication & collaboration** | Chama proposals and voting; moderated forums; classroom discussion prompts arising from shared crises |
| **Creativity & imagination** | Open-ended paths: learners design their own income mix (jobs, gigs, assets) |

**Subject connections:** Mathematics (percentages, interest, budgeting arithmetic),
Business Studies (income, expenditure, credit, insurance, investment), Social Studies /
Life Skills (responsibility, planning, resilience). **Values education:** responsibility,
integrity (honest odds and honest consequences), patriotism through authentic local
context, and social responsibility via cooperative finance.

**Assessment compatibility:** because every learner action produces recorded, quantified
outcomes (§22), the game supports the CBC's emphasis on observable competency evidence
over recall testing.

## 21. Age-Appropriateness & Content Safety

- **Money:** all in-game currency is virtual. There is no mechanism to deposit, win,
  cash out or transfer real money through gameplay. The only real-money transaction on
  the platform is an optional subscription paid by a parent/school via M-Pesa.
- **No gambling mechanics.** No loot boxes, no chance-based purchases, no mechanic where
  wagering produces gameplay advantage. (Design policy: any future chance-based minigame
  must display true odds and be framed as an explicit anti-gambling lesson.)
- **Age-banded content.** Bills, life events, jobs, taglines and reading level differ by
  the four age bands; an 8–12 player never receives adult obligations (mortgages, payday
  lending scenarios).
- **Language:** Kenyan-English with light Kiswahili flavour quotes; no profanity; crisis
  and hardship events are written factually, never fearfully.
- **Social safety:** no private direct messaging between players; forums are level-gated
  and admin-moderated; leaderboards show game identities.
- **Data minimisation:** name, email, DOB-derived age band and gameplay statistics only.
  No location tracking, no contacts access, no advertising, no data sale (non-profit
  publisher).
- **Screen-time posture:** the game is designed for *short daily sessions* (collect pay,
  pay bills, one quest) rather than long continuous play; daily caps and time-gated
  events reinforce that rhythm.

## 22. Observing & Measuring Learning

Every learner's play produces a durable, inspectable record. For a teacher, evaluator or
researcher, the following are available per learner:

| Evidence | Where | What it indicates |
|---|---|---|
| Credit score + full change history | Life HQ | Obligation management over time |
| Net worth and its composition | Life HQ / dashboard | Saving & investing behaviour |
| Courses completed | Career page | Content actually studied |
| Jobs held, salary collected on time | Career page | Consistency, reliability |
| Quests completed (each = a competency) | Quest board | Goal completion by topic |
| Savings statement (deposits vs interest) | Bank | Understanding of compounding |
| Life Story timeline | /life/timeline | The full narrative incl. crisis response |
| Badges & chapter reached | Profile | Milestone summary |

Suggested classroom protocol for a pilot: pre/post financial-literacy questionnaire,
plus weekly ten-minute reflections using each learner's timeline ("What happened in your
city this week? What would you do differently?"). The shared crisis events (§14) make
excellent whole-class debrief moments.
